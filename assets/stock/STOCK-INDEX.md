## Aesthetic findings — what the current Japanese-bakery editorial look actually is

**Ground, not white.** Almost nothing sits on pure white. The field is warm cream / paper — roughly `#F7F3EC`–`#EFE7DA` — often with a faint paper grain. Pure white appears only *inside* product cards, which makes the cutouts pop. Charcoal (`#1A1714`) and a single warm accent (terracotta, deep red, or amber) do all the emphasis work; a second accent is rare.

**Type is the whole design.** The dominant move is one very large high-contrast serif or didone display line — often set 2–3 lines, sometimes running off-canvas — paired with a tiny, wide-tracked uppercase sans for labels, prices and eyebrows. Body copy stays small and quiet. Script/handwriting appears once per page at most, as a warm signature above the headline. Japanese kana or a bilingual stack is used decoratively for authenticity, never as the primary voice.

**Composition is grid + float.** Two patterns repeat: (1) a strict 3–4 column product grid where each item is a shadowless cutout on a pale tile, with name / one-line description / price stacked underneath; (2) a hero where a single product cutout floats on cream, with crumbs scattered around it as motion, and a circular badge or seal ("est. / handmade daily") pinned in a corner.

**Light is soft, warm, directional.** Photography is late-morning window light, low contrast, warm-golden, shallow depth. Linen, kraft, cooling racks and wooden boards are the recurring props. Nothing is styled to look "clean-studio-perfect" — a torn loaf, a crumb trail, a dusting of flour reads as premium here.

**One dark band per page.** The dark section is a deliberate rhythm break: full-bleed near-black or deep red, oversized white/cream type, one moody backlit product shot. It usually carries the CTA ("Let's bake", "Order online") and sits just before the footer.

**Practical instruction for build agents:** cream ground everywhere, charcoal type, one warm accent, one dark full-bleed band, generous whitespace, cutouts over cropped photos for products, and warm-window-light photography for anything that shows people or place.
