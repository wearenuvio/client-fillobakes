## 1. VEHICLES — the van is the shopfront

The whole brand lives on a moving box. These are the vehicles that already solved "how does a
delivery body carry a print identity" — Japanese three-wheelers and kei trucks for silhouette,
liveried delivery trucks for the graphic system.

### 1.1 Yamazaki Pan delivery truck — the single most load-bearing vehicle ref
A bread company painting a *shopfront* onto a box body: scalloped yellow awning graphic wrapping
the top edge, cream field, red skirt, fat katakana ヤマザキ as display type, and a red roundel
portrait doing the work of a logo. This is a van pretending to be a bakery, which is exactly
Fillo's problem statement.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3d/Yamazaki_Bread_Truck1_at_Fukaebashi.jpg (4032×2268)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Yamazaki_Bread_Truck1_at_Fukaebashi.jpg

### 1.2 Daihatsu Midget DKA, 1959 — the silhouette everyone recognises as "Shōwa"
Single headlamp, bubble cab, open cargo deck. The Midget IS the visual shorthand for postwar
Japanese small commerce. If Fillo ever draws a mascot vehicle, it draws this.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/36/Daihatsu_Midget_%28DKA%29_%2759_%281%29.jpg (4032×2268)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Daihatsu_Midget_(DKA)_%2759_(1).jpg

### 1.3 Mazda K360 — the three-wheeler as a rounded, friendly, printable form
Softer and more toy-like than the Midget; the body panels are big flat curved fields that take
a painted wordmark beautifully. Good study for where a brush-lettered mark sits on a curve.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d2/Mazda_K360_%2823031013501%29.jpg (4608×3072)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mazda_K360_(23031013501).jpg

### 1.4 Mazda T2000 three-wheel truck (Mazda Museum)
The big brother — a long-nose three-wheeler with a proper flat-sided cargo body. Shows the
generous side panel a Fillo livery would actually get to use.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/50/Mazda_T2000_three-wheel_truck_in_the_Mazda_Museum.jpg (4032×3024)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mazda_T2000_three-wheel_truck_in_the_Mazda_Museum.jpg

### 1.5 Toyota Toyoace, 1959 — cab-over proportions, period two-tone
The direct ancestor of the modern kei delivery van: flat face, high waistline, two-tone split
with the join line acting as a natural rule for the type to sit on.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b2/Toyota_Toyoace%2C_1959_%288535732831%29.jpg (2448×3264)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Toyota_Toyoace,_1959_(8535732831).jpg

### 1.6 Subaru Sambar Truck (3rd gen) — the actual kei-truck package
The realistic Bengaluru-equivalent chassis. Included as the plain, un-styled base so the moodboard
shows what the livery has to transform.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a6/Subaru_Sambar_Truck_3rd_gen.jpg (1430×930)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Subaru_Sambar_Truck_3rd_gen.jpg

### 1.7 Clover Sonoma 1950s Divco milk truck, still running today — retro livery on a live brand
Not Japanese, and that's the point: a contemporary dairy keeps a period Divco in service with a
clean modern wordmark, a hand-drawn cow mark, "Since 1916", and two sweeping stripes. Proof that
retro-liveried delivery reads as *credible heritage*, not costume, when the type is disciplined.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c4/Divco_Clover_Sonoma_Milk_Truck_Sonoma_2026.jpg (3854×2542)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Divco_Clover_Sonoma_Milk_Truck_Sonoma_2026.jpg

### 1.8 1957 Divco Model 13 milk truck — the archetype, unrestored-original livery
The definitive print-era delivery body: rounded nose, standing-driver cab, huge flat flanks
designed from the factory to be a billboard.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/bf/1957_DIVCO_Model_13_Milk_Truck_%2851838619796%29.jpg (5568×3712)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1957_DIVCO_Model_13_Milk_Truck_(51838619796).jpg

## 4. MERCH — objects that carry the mark off the van

### 4.1 Tenugui, brush-drawn single-colour with a seal in the corner
The whole Shōwa Press thesis in one object: one ink colour, a loose brush drawing, a hand-written
vertical caption, and a small red-square seal bottom-right acting as the signature. This is the
merch template — cheap cotton, one screen, infinite charm.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/04/%E7%BE%8E%E5%B1%B1%E8%8D%98%E6%89%8B%E6%8B%AD.jpeg (2640×1760)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:美山荘手拭.jpeg

### 4.2 Hanko + shuniku (red ink pad) — the stamp as a physical object
Round carved seal beside its open vermilion paste tin. Fillo's "pressed mark" needs to exist as a
real tool the shop owns, not just a vector. This is that tool photographed plainly.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8c/Round_seal_and_red_ink_pad.jpg (4000×3000)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Round_seal_and_red_ink_pad.jpg

### 4.3 Hanko vending kiosks, Osaka — brush lettering as retail signage
Two はんこ machines fronted in red/gold with an enormous white brush-stroked はんこ and a gold
ichimatsu check band. Shows the hanko *category* styled as a consumer product — useful if Fillo
sells a stamp set as merch.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/40/Hanko_Stamp_Vendor_at_Donki_Sennichimae.jpg (3456×4608)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hanko_Stamp_Vendor_at_Donki_Sennichimae.jpg

### 4.4 Furoshiki wrapping sequence — packaging with zero printed material
Four-up demonstration of a plain cloth becoming a carry-bag. The strongest sustainable-packaging
argument available to a bakery, and it makes the *cloth print* the entire brand surface.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2b/Furoshiki%2C_traditional_japanese_wrapping_technique_with_cloths_%281%29.jpg (5184×2920)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Furoshiki,_traditional_japanese_wrapping_technique_with_cloths_(1).jpg

### 4.5 19th-century furoshiki, Cooper Hewitt collection — the historic pattern language
Museum-grade scan of an antique wrapping cloth. Use for motif scale, indigo/undyed contrast, and
how a family crest sits on textile.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b6/Wrapping_Cloth_%28furoshiki%29_%28Japan%29%2C_19th_century_%28CH_18399509-2%29.jpg (3715×4096)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wrapping_Cloth_(furoshiki)_(Japan),_19th_century_(CH_18399509-2).jpg

### 4.6 Melon cream soda in a footed kissaten glass
The retro-glassware ref. Specific glass shape, wooden coaster, long spoon, wood counter — the
exact serve-ware grammar that signals kissaten without a single graphic element.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c8/%E6%B2%88%E3%81%BF%E3%83%A1%E3%83%AD%E3%83%B3%E3%82%AF%E3%83%AA%E3%83%BC%E3%83%A0%E3%82%BD%E3%83%BC%E3%83%80_%2823087625323%29.jpg (1080×1080)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:沈みメロンクリームソーダ_(23087625323).jpg

### 4.7 Dagashi-ya no Yume museum — a full wall of Shōwa printed matter and tins
Hundreds of period wrappers, tins, banner strips, hanging paper flags and confectionery boxes in
one frame. This is the raw colour-and-print swatch library for the whole direction: saturated
red/yellow/blue, black outlines, dense type.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6d/Dagashi-ya-no-yume-museum1.jpg (3008×2000)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dagashi-ya-no-yume-museum1.jpg

### 4.8 Same museum, second angle — hanging pennant strings and product cards
Adds the *display system*: strings of printed pennants, price cards, and stacked tins. Good for
how Fillo dresses a stall or a van awning.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c3/Showa_no_Machi_05.jpg (2592×1944)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Showa_no_Machi_05.jpg

## 5. STREET — banners, boards, and the shopping arcade

### 5.1 Sumo nobori row — the definitive nobori reference
Rank on rank of banners in saturated flat green/orange/navy/yellow, each carrying vast
sumo-moji brush characters in red with a heavy white outline, topped by an ornate 贈 cartouche.
This is how you make lettering readable at 30 metres. Best single type-and-colour ref in this pass.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/58/%E5%8A%9B%E5%A3%AB%E3%81%AE%E3%81%BC%E3%82%8A_%284565499528%29.jpg (4272×2848)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:力士のぼり_(4565499528).jpg

### 5.2 Hand-written price cards on a shotengai bakery counter, Kyojima, Tokyo
Orange marker on cream card, underlined product name over a fat price, propped in a plastic stand
on lace paper, next to bagged koppe-pan. This is *exactly* the "hand-painted price board" cell —
and it is a bread counter, so it doubles as the shotengai bakery display.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3e/%E4%BA%AC%E5%B3%B6%E5%9C%B0%E5%9F%9F%E3%81%AE%E5%86%99%E7%9C%9FIMG_20221102_095309_07.jpg (2963×2963)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:京島地域の写真IMG_20221102_095309_07.jpg

### 5.3 Endōji Hommachi-dōri arcade, Nagoya — shotengai with a bakery on the corner
Covered arcade, a bread shop ("シマパン シバタ") under a striped awning with a phone number set in
the signboard, and a repeating row of identical blue banner flags marching down the arcade spine.
Shows banner *repetition* as an identity device.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/26/Endoji-hommachi-dori.jpg (2048×1536)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Endoji-hommachi-dori.jpg

### 5.4 Shōwa no Machi, Bungotakada — a preserved Shōwa shopping street
Hand-painted shop signboards in flat pink/cream/mint, enamel たばこ signs, a short noren over the
counter, an oval backlit sign, and a three-wheeler parked in the frame. A complete Shōwa retail
environment in one shot.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6e/Showa_no_Machi_03.jpg (2592×1944)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Showa_no_Machi_03.jpg

### 5.5 Shōwa Retro Goods Museum, Ōme — shopfront with nobori, boards and painted posters
Tiled roof, carved gold-on-brown horizontal signboard, hand-painted film posters flanking it,
paper lanterns, a cluster of nobori, and chalk/marker A-boards at street level. This is the
"old shop announces itself" stack Fillo should copy for a stall setup.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b7/%E6%98%AD%E5%92%8C%E3%83%AC%E3%83%88%E3%83%AD%E5%95%86%E5%93%81%E5%8D%9A%E7%89%A9%E9%A4%A8_2015_%2822057046293%29.jpg (4225×2799)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:昭和レトロ商品博物館_2015_(22057046293).jpg

### 5.6 Chindon'ya advertising troupe, Ajinomoto shop promotion (archival)
Street advertising before print media: towering vertical banners with brush kanji, a walking
bottle-shaped billboard costume, fans and drums. The historic answer to "how does a small shop
make noise on a street" — directly relevant to a van that has to announce itself on arrival.
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/aa/Advertisement_by_Chindon%27ya.jpg (2894×1979)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Advertisement_by_Chindon%27ya.jpg

### 5.7 Chindon'ya at a shop opening, Yamura-chō, Yamanashi, c. 1935
Sepia archival plate: the troupe lined up in front of a shop under painted signboards and flags on
opening day. The origin story image for "moving shop as an event".
IMG: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/09/Kobayashi_store_opening_celebration_Chindonya._Yamura_cho%2C_Yamanashi_Prefecture_in_1935_circa.jpg (4134×2692)
Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kobayashi_store_opening_celebration_Chindonya._Yamura_cho,_Yamanashi_Prefecture_in_1935_circa.jpg
