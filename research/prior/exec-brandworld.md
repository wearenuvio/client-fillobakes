# 1. MASCOT / CHARACTER ("the pet")

## 1a. The evidence that characters work — and it is unusually strong

This is not a taste question. Recurring brand characters are one of the few branding devices with hard effectiveness data behind them.

**System1 × IPA Effectiveness Databank** — the core citation **[Sourced]**. System1 tagged 300+ IPA-award campaigns back to 1992 for the presence of a "fluent device" (a fictitious character, human or creature, used as the primary creative vehicle across **more than one** ad):

| Effect | Campaigns with a fluent device vs without |
|---|---|
| More likely to **increase market share** | **+37%** |
| More likely to **increase customer gains** | **+27%** |
| More likely to **increase profit gains** | **+30%** |

Two secondary findings matter as much as the headline:
- **Character fluent devices generate larger, longer and broader effects** than non-character devices, and can persist in memory **for decades after the campaign ends** **[Sourced]**.
- **Almost nobody is doing it.** Fluent-device usage among IPA entrants fell from **41% (1992) to 12%**; US ads using the tactic sit at **~4%** **[Sourced]**. In a Bengaluru bakery category where nobody has a character, the whitespace is total.

The load-bearing word is **recurring**. A one-off illustration is not a fluent device. The value comes from the same character appearing across many executions over years — which is a content-discipline commitment, not a design purchase.

## 1b. What the reference cases actually teach

**The recurring cost nobody budgets for.** A character is a **subscription, not a purchase**. Budget **₹8,000–20,000/month [Est.]** for an illustrator on retainer producing 4–8 character assets a month, or the character dies in six weeks and you have bought an expensive sticker.

**When it dates badly — the documented failure modes [Sourced]:**
1. **Designed once, never revisited** → "a mascot designed once and never revisited will eventually feel dated."
2. **Built on a momentary trend** → "chasing trends or designing based on momentary cultural buzz results in a mascot with limited longevity."
3. **Redesign risk cuts the other way too** — when reviving a character with accumulated equity, getting the retain/change judgement wrong loses everything that made it valuable.
4. **Values mismatch surfaces later** — Joe Camel is the canonical case: the character became the vector for the backlash.
5. **A character with no defined behaviour becomes an irritant** — cited example: reflexive audience hatred for characters that exist without a reason to exist.

The counter-rule from the same literature: **the best characters evolve subtly while keeping the recognisable traits**, are grounded in brand values rather than trends, and have **strictly defined behavioural traits** written down before launch.

## 1f. RECOMMENDATION — **mascot now, but a specific, cheap, staged version**

**Verdict: yes to a character, no to a mascot programme.** Commission the character now; do not commission a mascot *system* (3D turnarounds, animation, plush) until it has earned it.

**The recommendation, precisely:**

> **Name the van and give it a face.** The character is **the van** — a small, constant, front-of-vehicle face (headlight eyes, a stamp-red mouth) plus a proper name, treated as a member of the team. The loaf-with-personality becomes a secondary supporting character used inside content, not the primary mark.

**Why this and not the others:**
1. It sits on the one asset no competitor has — a real vehicle with a live GPS feed already on the site.
2. It makes every existing content stream character content **for free**: every route photo, every doorstep video, every tracker screenshot already features the mascot.
3. Kuroneko is the proof that a delivery character riding a vehicle can outlive everything else in the identity (unchanged since 1957) **[Sourced]**.
4. It fails gracefully. If the character doesn't land, you've spent ~₹40k and the van is still a branded van. A plush programme fails at 100× that cost.
5. It gives a child at an apartment gate a reason to pull a parent outside — which is the actual growth mechanic of the society-gate route model.

**Staging:**

| Stage | Trigger | Spend **[Est.]** |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage 1 — now** | Character design + name + a written personality bible (5 traits, 3 things it never does, its voice in WhatsApp) + a 12-pose sticker sheet | **₹40,000–80,000** one-time + **₹8,000–15,000/mo** illustrator retainer |
| **Stage 2 — at ~90 days**, if the weekly character post consistently out-performs product posts | Physical van-front/roof figure; character on box seal and stamp lockup; character-voiced WhatsApp | **₹15,000–40,000** |
| **Stage 3 — only if customers ask for it unprompted** | Enamel pin → then plush. Never plush first (see §4) | See §4 |
| **Never (or much later)** | Walking costume (₹25,000–28,000 **[Sourced]**), animation rig, licensed IP collab | — |

**Two hard rules borrowed from the failure literature [Sourced]:**
- Write the **behaviour bible before the artwork**. A character without defined behaviour is the documented path to irritation.
- Commit to **recurrence**. System1's effect only exists across more than one execution. If Fillo cannot commit to ~4 character assets a month for a year, do not start.

# 2. NAMING SYSTEM

## 2a. The asset Fillo already has, and doesn't know it

The product catalogue already runs a **coherent city-fusion naming system**: Kyoto Curry, Seoul Spice, Calcutta Blaze, Bangalore Bloom, Mawa Melt, Korean Whisper, Taipei Whisper. That is a real naming *system* — an ownable rule (world city + sensory verb/noun), not a list of names. It is currently undocumented and unenforced, which is why nothing else in the brand (the drop, the plan, the van, the routes) is named to the same standard.

**The naming-system principle to fix in writing:** names are distinctive brand assets — non-brand-name elements that trigger the brand into memory **[Sourced]**. A weekly ritual with a name is recallable and searchable; "this week's specials" is neither.

## 2b. Precedents worth copying

| Precedent | The lesson |
|---|---|
| **Shinkansen service names** — Nozomi / Hikari / Kodama ("echo"), chosen by **public contest**; 550,000 people voted in a two-month window for the 1964 naming **[Sourced]** | Two lessons: (a) Japanese transport culture names the *service tier*, not just the route — directly transferable to drop tiers and membership levels; (b) **crowdsourcing the name is itself the campaign**. Fillo naming its van by public vote on Instagram costs ₹0 and buys a fortnight of participation |
| **Virgin Atlantic** — every aircraft has a name and a personality; Branson's stated motive was that registrations were impersonal, starting with "Maiden Voyager"; names honour iconic British women **[Sourced]** | A fleet naming *theme* (not one-off names) is what makes vehicle naming legible as a system when vehicle #2 arrives |
| **Southwest Airlines** — named/liveried aircraft (Lone Star One, Shamu One/Two/Three); 12 state-tribute aircraft **[Sourced]** | Numbered naming (`-One`, `-Two`) scales trivially. A "Fillo-go / Ichi-go, Ni-go" pattern is the Japanese-idiom version of exactly this |
| **Kuroneko Yamato / takkyūbin** — the company **trademarked its service name** and actively defends it against genericisation **[Sourced]** | Name the *service*, not just the company. The delivery ritual is the trademarkable thing |
| **Hotplate "drops"** — a drop is a preorder window with a defined start and end; subscribers get an SMS with the link, then a visible countdown before orders open **[Sourced]** | The category's own vocabulary for the weekly ritual is already "drop." Fillo should name its specific drop, not invent a new word for the format |
| **Amul Girl / HOG / MUG** — brand communities routinely carry member names (Harley Owners Group; Mac User Groups), and named-community members demonstrably spend more on merch and accessories **[Sourced]** | "Fillowers" (already live in Fillo's IG highlights) is a real asset. Formalise it — don't let it stay a highlight cover |

## 2d. Naming rules to write into the charter **[Est. — our design]**

1. **One system, one rule.** City + sensory word for products. Japanese common-noun for rituals and services. Human/place name + `-go` for vehicles. Never mix registers within a tier.
2. **Sayable over a van window.** If a delivery rider can't say it to a stranger at a gate in one breath, it fails.
3. **Searchable.** At least one name in the system should carry the category keyword (the `/shokupan` page already proves this works for Fillo).
4. **Trademark the service name, not just the brand name** (takkyūbin precedent) — the drop name and the plan name are the two worth clearing.
5. **Crowdsource exactly one name — the van.** It's the one where public ownership adds meaning rather than diluting control.

## 3a. Effectiveness — the data worth quoting to a client

Combined with the Veritonic 77%-recall figure already in `exec-tertiary.md`, this is the best-evidenced cheap asset in the whole brand-world layer.

## 3b. The Mister Softee lesson — sound is ownable property

**[Sourced]** The tune was written in **1960 by Les Waas**, titled **"Mister Softee Jingle and Chimes."** It was written for a TV commercial, then **loaded onto the trucks** — first as a music box (which wore out within a year and started sounding terrible), then as a recording. It was **copyrighted in 1960**, Mister Softee holds a **federal trademark** on it, only franchisees may use it, and the company has fought **scores of lawsuits** against unauthorised trucks.

Three transferable lessons:
1. **The jingle preceded the truck.** It was made as content first, then migrated to the vehicle. That is exactly the sequencing recommended for Fillo — make the motif for Reels, and only later consider whether it belongs on the van.
2. **A sound can be owned.** A 3-note mark is registrable in India as a sound mark (Yahoo's yodel was India's first sound-mark registration) — worth noting to the client, though not worth spending on in year one **[background, not separately sourced here]**.
3. **Mechanical playback degrades.** The music-box failure is a real ops warning against a cheap mounted mechanism.

## 3c. The three assets to build, specified

| Asset | Spec **[Est. — our design]** | Where it plays |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The bell** (already recommended in `exec-tertiary.md`) | Brass hand bell, **two rings**, always two, never three. Two rings is the sonic signature; the bell is just the instrument | Doorstep and gate, every stop, every time |
| **2. The 3-note mark** | Rising 3-note motif, ≤1.5 seconds, hummable by a child, playable on the bell's pitch so bell and motif are audibly the same brand. Deliver in 3 lengths: **0.8s sting** (Reel end-card), **1.5s logo** (site/tracker), **10s bed** (Shorts intro) | Every Reel and Short, the van-tracker page, the site's drop countdown |
| **3. The notification sound** | A single note from the motif — the *first* note, not the whole phrase. Used at order-confirm and van-approaching alerts | WhatsApp/PWA alerts, in-app confirmations |

**Design rule from the nostalgia literature [Sourced, `exec-tertiary.md` §4c]:** short, repetitive melodic phrases are retained more easily — which is why jingles loop rather than develop. Three notes, not eight bars.

# 4. MERCH & COLLECTIBLES

## 4a. The principle: merch is a demand test, not a revenue line

Merchandise carries a low COGS and can be genuinely profitable, and limited editions are what sell out (Panera's BAGguette, Miller Lite's Beercracker are the cited cases) — but the operative rule in the same literature is that **customer demand should guide what merch gets made**, and merch must fit the brand image rather than exist because a brand wanted merch **[Sourced]**. Tatte's tote/sweatshirt line is the workable bakery-scale model **[Sourced]**.

For a micro-bakery, the honest hierarchy is: **stickers → posters → pin → tote → apparel → plush.** Cost and inventory risk rise monotonically down that list; so does the amount of customer pull required to justify it.

## 4b. Item-by-item, with India costs

## 4c. What tiny brands actually sell vs vanity

**Actually sells (evidence-backed or structurally sound):**
- **Stickers** — free, not sold. Their return is collection behaviour and kid pull, not revenue.
- **The tote, when it has a job.** A ₹250–400 bread tote that subscribers use for doorstep handoff is a *utility purchase*, not merch. This is the version that sells.
- **Limited editions that sell out** — the documented pattern **[Sourced]**. A drop poster numbered 1/50 outperforms an always-available poster.
- **The pin, at ₹250–350** — impulse price point, no sizing, no inventory decay, high margin.

**Vanity (skip in year one):**
- T-shirts and hoodies (size matrix = dead stock).
- Plush before mascot pull is proven (₹1.3–3.5L committed at MOQ, against a ₹200 product).
- Mugs/bottles (no brand fit for a bread van, and heavy).
- A "merch store" page before there are three products worth buying.
